MODULE 1 — QUALITY CONCEPTS

What Does "Quality" Really Mean?

Quality means meeting agreed product and process requirements, not merely passing the final inspection.

📐

Observable & Measurable Requirements

Define product requirements in clear, verifiable terms rather than vague descriptions:

  • Dimensions: Geometric tolerances, lengths, diameters, runout.
  • Function: Torque resistance, electrical conductivity, leak tightness.
  • Visual Criteria: Surface finish, absence of burrs, flash, or color variation.
  • Safety & Traceability: Zero sharp edges, barcode readability, lot integrity.
Critical Distinction: Differentiate Conformity (part meets specs right now) from Process Effectiveness (the process continues to meet specs predictably over time).
⚠️

The Changing-Risk Manufacturing Example

The Case: A CNC machined pin measures perfectly within drawing tolerance during final inspection.

However, after an operator changeover, cutting tool wear causes the offset to drift toward the upper control limit.

The Quality Reality: The product is conforming right now, while tomorrow's risk is continuously increasing! Passing the final inspection today does not prove the process is under control tomorrow.
MODULE 1 — QUALITY CONCEPTS

QA vs QC: What's the Real Difference?

Both are essential for manufacturing excellence, but their targets and mechanisms are fundamentally distinct.

🛡️

Quality Assurance (QA) • Prevent Problems

Orientation: Process-Oriented

Primary Mission: Prevents defects before they can happen in the line.

Core Focus: Planning, process capability, design standards, poke-yoke error-proofing, and robust operating procedures.

Industrial Example: Concrete mix design approval, poke-yoke guide pin installation, robot weld parameter validation.
🔍

Quality Control (QC) • Find Problems

Orientation: Product-Oriented

Primary Mission: Detects defects after work has been completed.

Core Focus: Physical inspection, dimension measurement, laboratory testing, sorting, and rework screening.

Industrial Example: Concrete slump test, coordinate measuring machine (CMM) check, rebar inspection, final continuity test.
The Golden Rule: QC finds the fire after it starts; QA builds fireproof walls so the fire never ignites!
MODULE 1 — DEFECT ESCAPE

The Three Lines of Defect Defense

Describe a defect's journey from creation at the machine to potential customer escape.

1

First Line: Prevention

Stop Generation at Source:

Prevent defect creation using correct qualified materials, rigid equipment maintenance, validated parameter windows, and foolproof poka-yoke mechanisms.

Goal: Zero defect generation.
2

Second Line: Detection

Catch In-Process Early:

Detect abnormal output immediately at or adjacent to the station with inline sensors, first-piece inspection, or automatic interlocks.

Goal: Immediate containment.
3

Third Line: Containment

Stop Customer Escape:

Physical barrier stopping defective lots from leaving the plant via quarantine segregation, final release audits, and end-to-end traceability.

Goal: Zero customer escape.
Manufacturing Packaging Example: A wrong label can be generated at printing (Line 1 weakness), detected by a barcode scanner at station 2 (Line 2 defense), and re-checked at packing (Line 3). If operators routinely bypass the barcode scanner, the second line collapses completely!
MODULE 1 — ROOT CAUSE LENS

Process Inputs & The 6M System Lens

Look beyond simplistic operator error to analyze the full production system architecture.

1

Man (People)

Skills, training records, competency certification, human factors, ergonomic fatigue, shift handover rigor, and accountability.

2

Machine

Tooling wear, machine repeatability, sensor health, safety interlocks, PM preventive maintenance schedules, and breakdown alerts.

3

Material

Supplier lot conformance, raw material spec drift, component identification, FIFO discipline, storage temperature/humidity, and lot segregation.

4

Method

Clear standard operating procedures (SOP), work instructions (WI), sequence validation, clamping standards, and takt time adherence.

5

Measurement

Gauge calibration status, Measurement System Analysis (Gage R&R), resolution, gauge positioning accuracy, and inspection frequency.

6

Mother Nature

Environmental cleanliness, dust, ambient temperature fluctuation, humidity condensation, lux lighting levels, and electrostatic discharge (ESD).

Industrial Case: An assembly defect suddenly spikes after night shift handover following a resin lot change. Both component dimensional tolerances (Material) and ambiguous setup notes (Method/Man) must be investigated together.
MODULE 1 — ECONOMIC IMPACT

Visible vs Hidden Quality Losses

Quantify true operational waste by identifying both obvious scrap and hidden indirect costs.

📉

Visible Quality Losses

Direct costs immediately tracked in standard factory accounting:

  • Direct Material Scrap: Discarded raw materials and components.
  • Direct Rework Labor: Man-hours spent repairing defective assemblies.
  • 100% Sorting Campaigns: Emergency screening labor on trapped inventory.
  • Unplanned Line Stoppages: Downtime on bottleneck machines.
  • Premium Freight: Air expediting to avoid OEM customer plant shutdown.
🔍

Less-Visible (Hidden) Losses

Indirect drain on engineering capability and factory velocity:

  • Supervision & Engineering Firefighting: Diverting engineers from kaizen.
  • Lost Production Capacity: Takt time loss on starved downstream stations.
  • Repeated Inspections & Buffer Buildup: Excess work-in-progress (WIP).
  • Erosion of Customer Trust: Downgraded vendor ratings and lost quotes.
  • Warranty Administration: Field claims, legal reviews, and recall exposure.
Financial Calculation Discipline: When calculating savings from quality improvements, distinguish avoided actual cash expenditure (e.g., documented reduction in overtime pay or scrap materials) from recovered theoretical capacity.
MODULE 2 — QUALITY COST

Cost of Quality (COQ) & The Quality Iceberg

COQ provides a common language for comparing investment in assurance with devastating failure losses.

🧊

The Cost of Poor Quality Iceberg

Above the Waterline (Visible ~15%):

Scrap, rework hours, standard warranty claims, in-line reject disposal.

Below the Waterline (Hidden ~85%):

Customer dissatisfaction, engineering firefighting, increased lead times, reduced line velocity, excess buffer inventory, supplier destabilization, and brand destruction.

⚖️

The Total Cost of Quality Framework

Total COQ splits into two fundamental categories:

1. Cost of Good Quality (CoGQ):
Investment made to do things right the first time.
• Prevention Costs + Appraisal Costs
2. Cost of Poor Quality (CoPQ):
Losses incurred because products failed requirements.
• Internal Failure Costs + External Failure Costs
MODULE 2 — QUALITY COST

The Four Cost of Quality Categories

Clear definitions and manufacturing shop-floor assignments for every cost element.

1

Prevention

Cost to prevent errors:

  • Operator training & certification
  • Poka-yoke tooling design
  • Design & Process FMEA studies
  • Process capability validation
  • Preventive maintenance of jigs
2

Appraisal

Cost to evaluate quality:

  • Receiving raw material audit
  • In-process coordinate inspection
  • First-piece sample verification
  • Laboratory material analysis
  • Measurement gauge calibration
3

Internal Failure

Defects caught before delivery:

  • Factory scrap & material dump
  • Component rework & repair
  • Re-testing failed lots
  • Sorting trapped inventory
  • Downtime due to defective feed
4

External Failure

Defects escaping to customer:

  • Customer reject returns
  • Field warranty claims
  • OEM plant sorting fees
  • Product recall response
  • Customer line shutdown claims
Concrete Example: Servicing a poka-yoke limit switch is Prevention; final inspector wages are Appraisal; scrapped stamped brackets are Internal Failure; warranty repairs for loose screws in dealership cars are External Failure.

Worked COQ Calculation & Benchmark Metrics

Use consistent accounting periods and avoid double counting when tracking monthly quality performance.

🧮

Mathematical Model & Formulas

COQ = Prevention + Appraisal + Internal Failure + External Failure

Total Failure Cost = Internal Failure + External Failure

Failure Cost Ratio = (Failure Cost / Total COQ) × 100%

To measure the financial benefit of a QA Network countermeasure, compare baseline expenditure to post-improvement values at comparable production volumes.

MODULE 2 — ECONOMIC DYNAMICS

Cost Curves: How Prevention Eliminates Failure Loss

Every dollar invested in robust prevention yields exponential reductions in appraisal and failure costs.

📈

The Economics of Early Prevention

As engineering effort and investment in Prevention (error-proofing, poka-yoke, training) increase, the occurrence of defects plummets.

  • Internal Failures: Rapidly drop as scrap and rework disappear.
  • External Failures: Drop toward absolute zero as defect generation is halted at the station.
  • Net Financial Result: Total operating cost declines sharply, far out-weighing the modest cost of prevention fixtures!
📉

The Trap of Relying Solely on Appraisal

Attempting to eliminate customer defects by adding more inspectors (Appraisal) results in skyrocketing labor costs without fixing root causes.

  • Inspection is never 100% effective (human visual fatigue misses 15–20% of defects).
  • Defective parts were still produced, wasting machine time and raw materials.
  • Appraisal costs rise steeply while failure risk remains high.
Strategic Takeaway: Move your budget out of appraisal and sorting into automatic station-level prevention!

MODULE 3 — QA NETWORK FUNDAMENTALS

What Is a QA Network?

A process-linked map of potential defects, operational impacts, and defense assurance mechanisms.

🌐

Core Architecture of the Network

The QA Network differs fundamentally from static tables:

  • Process-Connected Nodes: Each node represents an actual physical production step connected in true manufacturing sequence.
  • Defect Pathways: Shows exactly how an error is created, transferred downstream, detected, or allowed to escape.
  • Multi-Barrier Mapping: At each step, documents current occurrence prevention, detection barriers, reaction rules, and assigned owners.
🔄

Manufacturing Flow Example

Consider a precision machined mechanical bracket:

10. Receiving Inspection → 20. CNC Machining → 30. Deburring
40. Part Washing → 50. Dimensional Check → 60. Final Packing
The Pathway Danger: A burr generated at step 20 (Machining) can easily pass undetected through washing into step 60 and directly into the customer's assembly line! The QA Network reveals this exposure gap instantly.
MODULE 3 — PREREQUISITES

Three Core Implementation Requirements

Mandatory specifications required before initiating any QA Network deployment.

1

Implement Before Problems Occur

QA Network is not a post-mortem autopsy report! It must be executed proactively during pilot launch, line changeover, or engineering revisions to anticipate defects and seal escape routes before parts are shipped.

2

Establish a Cross-Functional Team

Quality cannot work in isolation. The QA Network team must include representatives from Production Operators, Process Engineering, Quality Assurance, and Maintenance.

3

Shop-Floor Ground Truth (Go & See)

Never complete a QA Network inside an office! Analysis must be based on actual, observable shop-floor conditions — Genchi Genbutsu (Go & See) — observing real cycles, real wear, and real operator handovers.

Summary: Proactive Timing + Multi-Disciplinary Team + Physical Shop-Floor Reality = Bulletproof Quality Network!
MODULE 3 — ESCALATION RULE

The 1-30-300-3000 Escalation Rule

Eliminate weaknesses at the machine before defects cascade down the supply chain.

🔺

The Defect Cost Escalation Hierarchy

Level A: Vehicle End User Ratio: 1
Level B: Vehicle Assembly Plant (OEM) Ratio: 30
Level C: Supplier Final Inspection / Pack Ratio: 300
Level D: Supplier In-Process Production Ratio: 3000
🛡️

Core Operating Strategy

If a defect escapes to Level A, the company faces field recalls, legal liability, and brand destruction.

If it reaches Level B, the OEM halts assembly lines, charging massive shutdown penalties and issuing formal supplier warning notices.

Action Strategy:
1. Do not wait for problems to occur at Level A or B.
2. Systematically eliminate weaknesses at Level C and Level D step by step!
MODULE 3 — PURPOSE & EVIDENCE

5 Core Objectives & Practical Inputs

Ground the quality network on credible engineering blueprints and physical evidence.

🎯

5 Core Objectives (Purpose)

  • 1. Identify & Assess: Discover all potential manufacturing defects and their consequences.
  • 2. Eliminate / Reduce: Lower the physical likelihood of occurrence at the source.
  • 3. Document Know-How: Preserve shop-floor operational wisdom for future lines.
  • 4. Continuous Improvement: Drive systematic PDCA problem prevention.
  • 5. Protect Downstream: Guarantee zero defective parts reach customers or next stations.
📋

Mandatory Practical Inputs

  • Process Flow & Control Plan: Current approved revisions.
  • Technical Blueprints: GD&T drawings and specification sheets.
  • Approved Work Instructions (WI): Point-of-use operator standards.
  • Defect Logs: Scrap records, customer feedback, and abnormal shift logs.
  • Agreed Severity Criteria: Calibrated definitions to prevent scoring inconsistencies.
Calibration Example: Two engineers score "improper bolt torque" very differently because one considers catastrophic safety impact while the other evaluates only simple rework cost. Agreeing on unified scoring rules before the review eliminates subjectivity!
MODULE 3 — BOUNDARY SETTING

Selecting Network Scope & Target Boundaries

Keep the analysis boundary meaningful enough for concrete, measurable engineering action.

🔍

When to Deploy QA Network

  • 1. New Products & Processes: Establish robust error-proofing during APQP / launch.
  • 2. Engineering Changes (4M Changes): When tooling, materials, or methods are modified.
  • 3. High-Risk / High-Loss Defect Families: Chronic scrap lines or customer complaints.
  • 4. Every Process Station: Quality assurance must be built into every operation, never limited to final packaging!
  • 5. Proactive Problem Prevention: Strengthening QA means preventing defects, not merely sorting them after they happen.
🔄

Managing Process Interfaces

Define exact start and end steps, rework loops, and outsourced operations. Never ignore outsourced interfaces (e.g. outsourced plating or heat treatment) where wrong material or traceability loss can occur.

Scope Example: Raw polymer receiving → drying → molding → pin insertion → electrical test → packing. Outsourced pin plating remains a critical incoming interface.
Mandatory Rule: Whenever a problem occurs in production, the corrective action must also be formally incorporated into the QA Network Sheet!

MODULE 3 — CONTINUOUS IMPROVEMENT

The PDCA Cycle Applied to QA Network

Plan, Do, Check, and Act to prove controls work and sustain quality gains permanently.

P

PLAN

Define the Problem:

  • State defect, station, period, and financial loss.
  • Confirm process boundary and suspected pathways.
  • Set measurable target, owner, and deadline.
D

DO

Pilot Improvements:

  • Trial error-proofing fixture on controlled scope.
  • Train operators and update point-of-use WI.
  • Record dates, part numbers, and test results.
C

CHECK

Prove Performance:

  • Compare normalized before/after defect rates.
  • Verify no new unintended failure modes arose.
  • Audit physical safeguard, not just paperwork!
A

ACT

Standardize & Replicate:

  • Update PFMEA and Control Plan standards.
  • Revise training syllabus for new hires.
  • Replicate proven poka-yoke across parallel lines.

STEP 1 • DEFECT IDENTIFICATION

Step 1: Review QC Flow & Identify Potential Defects

Identify potential defects based on actual shop-floor conditions and operational realities.

🔍

Two Classes of Potential Defects

2.1 Human-Related Defects:
Failure to follow the Work Instruction (WI), skipped operations, missing components, inverted part insertion, or assembling incorrect model variants.
2.2 Process-Related Defects:
Operating parameters drifting outside specification window, worn tooling, heater burnout, abnormal hydraulic pressure, or parts exceeding shelf life.
📌

5 Basic Assumptions for Defining Key Points

  • 1. Any Time Risk: A defect can occur at any time, even if it has never happened before in company history!
  • 2. Disregard Existing Controls: When brainstorming potential defects, do NOT consider current safeguards.
  • 3. In-Process Focus: Consider production operations; exclude final inspection unless the inspection itself can scratch or damage the product.
  • 4. Upstream Conformance: Assume parts arriving from preceding operations meet specifications.
  • 5. Voice of Customer: Review all downstream assembly complaints and customer field returns.
STEP 1 • DAMAGE ANALYSIS

Step 1: Damage Analysis & Severity Ranking Symbols

Sequence: Next Process → Customer (Vehicle Assembly) → Vehicle End Use. (Assume NO inspection by customer!)

Symbol Classification Engineering Definition & Real-World Example
S Safety / Accident Critical Defect could cause a vehicle accident, fire, fuel leakage, or loss of braking.
X Vehicle Stop (Non-moving) Defect in a stationary structural component causes vehicle to stop immediately (e.g., Steering knuckle fracture).
XA Vehicle Stop (Moving Part) Defect on a rotating/moving component causes immediate vehicle stop (e.g., Rear differential seizure).
XB Vehicle Stop (Leakage) Severe fluid leakage causes vehicle to shut down or fail completely.
R Regulation & Statutory Defect violates legal, safety, or homologation requirements in target country.
E Emissions / Environmental Component defect causes the vehicle to generate excessive emissions or environmental pollution.
V Customer Assembly Impact Defect detected during OEM customer assembly (e.g., crossmember cannot mount to frame); line claim.
A Moving Part Operates Defect on moving part, but vehicle can continue operating (e.g., abnormal differential gear whine).
B Non-moving Part Operates Vehicle continues operating after defect occurs (e.g., minor seepage around axle oil seal).
C Cosmetic / Appearance Aesthetic defect; vehicle operates normally (e.g., green corrosion stains on rear chrome bumper).

STEP 2 • OCCURRENCE CONTROLS

Step 2: Occurrence Prevention Analysis (Levels 1 to 4)

Process controls that prevent or reduce the generation of defective parts at the machine.

1

Level 1

Automatic Device-Based:

A prevention device is installed and prevents 100% of human- and process-related defects automatically (e.g. keyed fixture, electrical interlock).

Highest Reliability
2

Level 2

Person-Operated Device:

A prevention device is installed to prevent defects, but an operator must manually position or trigger it (e.g. manual torque click-wrench, guide template).

Device + Human
3

Level 3

Operator Prevention with WI:

No prevention device is installed. Prevention depends entirely on the operator correctly following standard Work Instructions (WI).

Human Reliance
4

Level 4

Operator Without Adequate WI:

No device provided; procedure is missing, unclear, not followed, or process inherently cannot prevent defects.

Uncontrolled Risk
Design Principle: Absolute priority must be given to Machine- or Equipment-based automated prevention (Level 1)!
STEP 2 • DETECTION BARRIERS

Step 2: Flow-Out Prevention Analysis (Levels 1 to 4)

Inspection or detection controls that stop defective products from reaching downstream processes or customers.

1

Level 1

Automatic Device-Based:

A detection device is installed and detects 100% of defects automatically with auto-reject or line interlock (e.g. vision camera, automated leak tester).

100% Auto Detection
2

Level 2

Person-Operated Device:

A detection device is available, but a person must operate the instrument (e.g. micrometer, dial indicator, manual pressure tester).

Instrument + Human
3

Level 3

Operator Detection with WI:

No detection device available; detection depends entirely on operator visual or tactile check following the inspection WI.

Human Inspection
4

Level 4

No Detection Available:

No detection device; procedure missing or defect physically undetectable before shipping.

Blind Escape Route
Detection Priority: Machine-automated inline detection (Level 1) eliminates human inspection fatigue and prevents defect flow-out with certainty.

MODULE 4 — FACTS & DEFECT DISCOVERY

Go & See (Genchi Genbutsu) & The 5 Gen Framework

Verify the actual place, item, observable facts, operating conditions, and engineering principles.

👀

Go & See: Observe the Real Workplace

Go directly to the workstation where the defect can be created or escape:

  • Prepare one focused observation question and follow an actual part through the complete cycle.
  • Observe critical transition states: startup, changeover, line restart, material replenishment, and abnormal handling.
  • Compare actual operator practice against current posted instructions.
  • Speak with operators without blame; separate raw observation from speculation.
⛩️

The 5 Gen Observation Framework

  • 1. Genba (現場): The actual shop floor where work occurs.
  • 2. Genbutsu (現物): The actual physical part, tooling, or machine.
  • 3. Genjitsu (現実): The actual facts, measurements, and operating state.
  • 4. Genri (原理): The underlying scientific or engineering principle.
  • 5. Gensoku (原則): The standard rules, parameters, and specifications.
Press Station Example: Inspect cracked part (Genbutsu), record stamping force (Genjitsu), observe die seating (Genba), review metal flow dynamics (Genri), compare to setup standard (Gensoku).
MODULE 4 — OBJECTIVE TRUTH

Good Evidence vs Assumptions & Defect Pathways

An assessment is only as reliable as its supporting facts. Map how defects travel between stations.

⚖️

Testable Observations vs Opinions

Weak Opinion (Assumption):
"The operators do not care and fail to follow the Work Instruction."
Strong Fact (Testable Evidence):
"On 14 October, during 3 of 5 observed cycles, step 4 was skipped; obsolete Revision B was posted while Revision C was released in the system."

Direct physical samples, calibrated gauge logs, and traceable reject bins are indisputable compared to verbal statements.

🗺️

Mapping Defect Creation & Escape

Trace how defective material could travel between workstations:

  • Mark exact creation origin, transfer mechanisms, and inspection gates.
  • Record barrier failure conditions: bypass switches, sample rate gaps, and false acceptance thresholds.
  • Account for shift changes, mixed component bins, and line clearances.
Connector Case: Missing pin is generated at insertion; electrical tester detects continuity, but cannot verify secondary latch engagement!
MODULE 5 — RISK EVALUATION

Risk Priority: Severity (S1–S4) × Weakness (W1–W4)

Use severity together with control weakness to prioritize engineering resources.

S

Severity Rating (S1 to S4)

  • S1 (Minor): Small localized inconvenience; no functional impact.
  • S2 (Moderate): Significant internal rework; controlled disruption.
  • S3 (Major): Customer line stoppage; serious functional failure or complaint.
  • S4 (Critical): Plausible safety hazard, crash risk, or regulatory violation.
W

Control Weakness (W1 to W4)

  • W1 (Robust): Verified automatic interlock or reliable near-source poka-yoke.
  • W2 (Adequate): Defined and evidenced control with some human/sampling reliance.
  • W3 (Weak): Inconsistent coverage, delayed feedback, poor calibration.
  • W4 (Absent): No barrier exists, or routine bypass is practiced.
P

Priority Score (P = S × W)

Score: 1 to 16
• 1–3: Routine Monitoring
• 4–7: Planned Improvement
• 8–11: High Priority
• 12–16: Urgent Escalation
Safety Override Rule: Any S4 safety risk or actual customer escape requires immediate escalation regardless of score!
MODULE 5 — CONTAINMENT & RECURRENCE

Assessing Detection Containment & Preventing Recurrence

Correcting one incident today is not the same as fixing the system to prevent repetition tomorrow.

⏱️

The Hidden Risk of Sampling Gaps

Evaluate inspection location, method, coverage, independence, and reaction latency:

The 59-Minute Exposure Gap:
An hourly 1-piece sampling inspection leaves 59 minutes of production completely exposed! If tool drift starts at minute 05, up to 55 minutes of defective parts have moved downstream before detection.

Automated 100% inline presence verification eliminates this gap completely. Challenge sensors daily using known defective master samples.

🛡️

Containment vs True Recurrence Prevention

Emergency Containment:
Sorting 4,000 finished parts removes known bad units today. This fixes the immediate batch, but fixes zero root causes.
Systemic Recurrence Prevention:
A verified mechanical guide fixture and automatic interlock prevent the defect from being physically created ever again!

Always verify root causes using physical evidence, update engineering standards, revise training, and conduct audit checks.

MODULE 6 — SHOP-FLOOR CASES

Industrial Cases 1 & 2: Injection Molding & Stamping

Real-world manufacturing applications showing defect creation, transfer, and barriers.

🧪

Case 1: Plastic Connector Injection Molding

  • Drying: Wet resin causes visual splay and brittle locking tabs → Countermeasure: Automated dew-point moisture interlock.
  • Molding: Short shots and flash → Countermeasure: Cavity pressure sensor and mold temperature limits.
  • Pin Insertion: Bent or unseated pins → Countermeasure: Optical pin-height sensor and 100% electrical continuity check.
  • Packing: Label/part mix-up → Countermeasure: Barcode scan matching carton label to tote RFID.
⚙️

Case 2: Progressive Metal Stamping & Machining

  • Stamping: Punch wear generates excessive sharp burrs → Countermeasure: Tonnage monitor and stroke-count die maintenance schedule.
  • Deburring: Vibratory deburring hides root cause burrs → Countermeasure: First-off deburring check with go/no-go ring gauge.
  • CNC Milling: Hole diameter drift due to tool wear → Countermeasure: Contact tool touch-probe after every 50 cycles.

Industrial Cases 3 & 4: Welding & Packaging Traceability

Addressing critical safety joints and high-frequency label mix-ups.

🔥

Case 3: Robotic Welding & Bracket Assembly

The Hazard: Incomplete fusion and weld porosity inside a suspension bracket joint.

  • Creation: Clamp incorrectly positioned before robotic spot weld cycle.
  • Barrier Failure: Visual inspection after welding cannot reveal internal penetration defects!
  • QA Network Action: Install proximity interlocks ensuring parts seat flush before robot start; implement ultrasonic testing at first-off.
📦

Case 4: Packaging & Lot Traceability

The Hazard: Incorrect part number label applied to shipping cartons.

  • Creation: Two adjacent conveyor bins hold visually identical connectors; a shared zebra printer allows selection of either label.
  • QA Network Action: Physical partition separating part bins; barcode reader interlock requiring carton scan before label prints; strict line clearance protocol.

Action Plan Execution, Leading KPIs & Escalation

Building Testable Action Plans

Vague Action (Fails Audit): "Improve operator training and tell workers to pay attention."
Testable Action (World-Class): "Install keyed nest fixture, validate with 20 deliberate misloads, revise WI with color photos, and audit daily for 4 weeks."
